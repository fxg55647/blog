The opposite shore is visible from your window, but driving there requires a long detour. I propose a network of small electric robot ferries carrying groceries, parcels, and tools over short water crossings. The vessels would not need to operate in every weather or throughout the year. They would provide inexpensive shortcuts when conditions were good enough and remain docked otherwise.

*Concept draft: the vessel, routes, and costs have not been designed or tested. The calculation below is an imaginary comparison scenario.*

## A limited service instead of a perfect ferry

The idea starts with the service commitment. A small water shortcut would not be intended to replace a bridge, a year-round island ferry, or an essential transport service that must work in difficult weather. A limited service could suspend operations proactively.

That could allow a simpler and cheaper implementation. Savings would nevertheless not follow merely from calling the vessel a robot. Working docks, charging, cargo handling, maintenance, and a way to assist a vessel in trouble would also be necessary.

The first application would be goods transport. Passenger transport could be investigated separately later. A successful cargo route would not itself establish that the same vessel or operating model was suitable for passengers.

## One vessel could serve several needs

In the morning, the vessel could deliver groceries to an island collection dock. Its return trip could carry a fisher's catch or small consignments. Later, the same capacity could carry tools, spare parts, or farming supplies.

Shared use would matter because a vessel waiting for just one infrequent task might see too little activity. Orders would need combining so transport remained useful without every customer requiring an individual departure. An inexpensive water route does not help if getting goods to the dock costs more than the entire road detour.

The network could be based on a common vessel design and similar docks, charging points, and remote supervision. Each additional route would still need a local assessment. A short distance on a map says little about waves, other traffic, or cargo handling arrangements.

## Stopping safely would be part of the service

An autonomous or remotely supervised vessel would need predefined responses to deteriorating weather, communication loss, and technical failure. When is a departure cancelled, where does an interrupted vessel go, and who attends when needed? These would be basic service questions.

Users should be told clearly when the route is available and what the alternative is. If the purpose is to make daily life easier, waiting for an uncertain delivery must not create another inconvenience. Limited availability could be acceptable if understood in advance.

## Novelty

I searched solutions and earlier discussions using terms including *autonomous electric ferry*, *small cargo ferry*, *island grocery delivery*, and *waterway logistics*. Small electric autonomous ferries and water shortcuts are not new basic ideas. Three counterparts come close to the proposal.

### Small autonomous ferries have already been tested

According to the [Callboats CAT 6 presentation](https://callboats.com/cat-6/), a six-passenger electric prototype operated commercially in Helsinki in summer 2020. The presentation mentions a supervising captain during passenger operations. This matters when imagining an “uncrewed ferry”: demonstrating automation does not necessarily mean a service without personnel aboard.

[Zeam's background account](https://www.zeam.se/en/about) describes the milliAmpere ferry tested on a short crossing in Trondheim and the development of small autonomous electric ferries. Short urban water routes are therefore already the subject of practical development.

### Island goods transport has already been piloted

Japan's transport ministry [Smart Island project account](https://smartisland.mlit.go.jp/demo/33island/) describes an autonomous electric vessel trial combining cargo and passenger transport between Ōsakikamijima and Ikunoshima. It included grocery deliveries and testing remote supervision. The account also identifies commercial viability and implementation arrangements as further questions.

This is a very close counterpart, so combining goods and passenger transport cannot be presented as a new invention either. My proposal would emphasize deliberately limited operating conditions and replicating a common underlying system across small routes.

### Discussions and research also reveal limitations

The [2020 Roboat II study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10220) examines an autonomous vessel for urban environments and navigation experiments in confined waterways. It provides technical background, but does not demonstrate the viability of the route network proposed here.

A [2020 Reddit discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/Futurology/comments/jhabni) also considers autonomous ferries and how few suitable crossings there might be. It is not technical research, but shows that both the idea and the question of appropriate locations have already been discussed publicly. Based on these searches, I would describe this as a proposed operating model for existing approaches, rather than new vessel technology.

## Scale of the problem and potential of the solution

The comparison is not a world in which transport continues unchanged forever. Electric cars, combined deliveries, and existing water services can already reduce costs. A robot ferry's additional benefit would be only the remaining detour its route actually eliminates beyond those improvements.

### An imaginary route would avoid 80,000–200,000 road kilometres

Suppose a route operates on 200 days a year and replaces 20–50 separate road journeys daily. Assume each replacement removes 20 kilometres after accounting for trips to docks and onward delivery. These are my scenario assumptions, not observations about an identified route or demand.

Avoided driving would then be **200 × 20–50 × 20 = 80,000–200,000 kilometres annually**. The calculation requires journeys genuinely disappearing: if groceries would already travel in one delivery van, they cannot be counted as separate car trips.

As a monetary reference, consider [Finland's 2026 kilometre allowance of €0.55 per kilometre](https://www.vero.fi/tietoa-verohallinnosta/uutishuone/uutiset/uutiset/2025/verovapaat-matkakustannusten-korvaukset-vuonna-2026/). Applied here, the cost comparison for the driving being replaced would be **€44,000–110,000 annually**. However, this allowance is not a measurement of avoided marginal costs: a car's fixed costs do not disappear when one journey is eliminated. The figure should therefore not be called realized savings.

### Vessel costs would determine whether anything remained

The full annual cost of the water service would have to be deducted from that reference: capital costs for the vessel and docks, energy, supervision, maintenance, insurance, and cargo handling. No quotations or adequate cost data are available here. Net savings or a payback period therefore cannot yet be justified.

The biggest source of uncertainty is how many separate detours would actually disappear. Operating days and human labor requirements could also change the result completely. The first assessment should examine one route, count actual deliveries, and obtain implementation cost estimates. A global monetary figure would offer false precision at this stage.

### New activity could emerge where transport currently prevents it

Beyond savings, a water shortcut could enable things not currently done: delivering a small harvest to a buyer, sharing a tool across the water, or extending a service to an island. These would be new opportunities and must not be counted again as avoided car journeys.

Water could thereby become a connection rather than a barrier in daily life. The key would be finding locations where a short crossing, sufficient use, and a manageable service commitment coincide. One vessel would not need to transform all transport if it made one useful shortcut work.
