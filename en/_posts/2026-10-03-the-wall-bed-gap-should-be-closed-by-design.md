In many Finnish daycare centres, children take their naps in beds that are
folded up against the wall at the end of the day, a bit like the door of a
cupboard. In spring 2026 in Vantaa, nobody noticed that one child was still
asleep in a bed as it was being raised. The child slid into the gap between
the bed and the wall and died. So far the response has been twofold: staff
are instructed not to raise the beds while children are present, and the
most dangerous models are being replaced. I propose a third option: close the
gap itself with a flexible fabric that can hold a child's weight and moves
with the bed. Then a single adult's momentary mistake would no longer be
enough to cause an accident.

## What happened in Vantaa and what followed

According to the Safety Investigation Authority (Otkes) and news reports,
staff at the daycare centre began raising the wall beds after nap time, and
nobody noticed that one child was still in bed. The investigation found no
mechanical defect in the bed, but Otkes concluded that wall beds pose a
serious safety risk that had not been identified in daycare settings
([Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20217443)). The two-year-old child later died in
hospital ([Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20218931)).

The core of the risk is the structure. According to news reports, the bed's
mechanism allows a child lying in it to slide into the gap between the bed
and the wall as the bed is raised, after which the bed's position can no
longer be changed
([MTV Uutiset](https://www.mtvuutiset.fi/artikkeli/juuri-nyt-vantaa-poistaa-paivakotiensa-vaaralliset-sangyt-kaytosta/9351670)).
It was not the only case: the Finnish Safety and Chemicals Agency (Tukes) has
compiled five cases from 2015–2025, three in which a child was caught between
the bed and the wall and two involving fingers in the hinges, with beds from
different manufacturers ([Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20217619)).

### Vantaa did not remove all its beds, only the risky models

The most interesting detail is Vantaa's own review. The city went through
more than 12,000 beds and about 15 bed models. Most were judged to carry no
serious risk even if a child were accidentally in the bed while it was
raised. Only the models with the risky structure are being replaced, at an
estimated cost of about €220,000 ([Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20230874)).
Helsinki, by contrast, is giving up all 1,447 of its wall beds in 99 daycare
centres
([STT Info](https://www.sttinfo.fi/tiedote/72306958/helsinki-luopuu-paivakodeissa-kaappisangyista)).
At the same time, the official line has emphasised correct use: beds are
opened and closed only when no children are in the room
([Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20218306)).

Vantaa's finding says the danger is not in wall beds as such but in a
particular structure. And if the danger is in the structure, the structure
can also be fixed.

## The idea: physically close the gap with a flexible barrier

If the dangerous chain of events is specifically that a child slides over
the mattress or the end of the bed into the gap between the bed and the
wall, a structural fall barrier could break the chain entirely.

### A cargo net is the right thought but the wrong product

The idea started from an ordinary cargo net. As such, it would not be the
best solution: a loose or wide-mesh net could create new risks of snagging,
strangulation or trapped limbs. Designed for the purpose, though, the same
idea is interesting. Options would be a very tight, fine-mesh safety net or,
preferably, a flexible continuous fabric or panel that covers the whole
dangerous gap and can hold a child's weight.

### The barrier must be taut, pocket-free and move with the bed

The barrier has three requirements. It must not form a pocket a child could
sink into. It must not get in the way of raising the bed. And it must work
every time without anyone having to remember anything. One implementation
would be a taut safety fabric attached to the bed, running between the end
or edge of the bed and a wall fixing, moving with the bed. It could be
retrofitted to the risky models.

### Human error should not be enough for a catastrophe

The question can be put like an engineer: why not fit the risky model with a
retrofittable fail-safe structure that makes getting into the gap between
the wall and the bed physically impossible? This is a classic problem: don't
try to stop people from making mistakes, design the machine so that a
mistake does not lead to catastrophe. Instructions and supervision are
needed, but they rely on a busy adult noticing a child sleeping under a
blanket every single time.

## Why the original designer did not account for this

I did not find a direct answer to this, so what follows is my own reasoning
based on public information.

### The bed was designed to be raised empty

The wall bed is originally a space-saving solution for the home, where an
adult raises their own, empty bed. The European standard EN 1129-1 covers
specifically the safety of the folding mechanism of foldaway beds, not the
bed's other uses
([Genorma](https://genorma.com/en/standards/pren-1129-1-rev)). From the
mechanism's point of view, what matters is that the bed does not fall or hit
the user. A situation where a small child is inside the bed at the moment it
is raised falls outside that way of thinking.

### At a daycare centre, the use case is different

At a daycare centre, one adult may raise many beds in a row, the children are
small and hard to see under blankets, and raising the beds is routine. The
same product moved into an environment where a mistake is far more likely,
but its structure stayed that of a home bed.

### Rare near misses never piled up on anyone's desk

The cases known to Tukes were spread over ten years, different cities and
different manufacturers, and Tukes took no action based on them
([Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20217619)). When a single manufacturer sees at
most one case every few years, the risk looks theoretical. According to
Otkes, the hazard had not been identified in daycare settings.

## Novelty

I searched for counterparts in Finnish and English with search terms such as
*kaappisänky turvallisuus lisävaruste* (wall bed safety accessory), *wall
bed child entrapment*, *murphy bed safety interlock*, *bed wall gap
entrapment barrier* and *hospital bed gap filler*. The closest counterparts:

- **Entrapment zones in hospital beds.** In 2004 the US Food and Drug
  Administration (FDA) published draft guidance naming seven zones of a
  hospital bed where a patient can become trapped. It was based on 575
  reported cases over 19 years, 358 of them fatal
  ([Infection Control Today](https://infectioncontroltoday.com/view/fda-issues-guidance-hospital-bed-design-reduce-patient-entrapment)).
  Measuring and filling gaps is thus established practice in healthcare,
  but it applies to stationary beds, not ones that fold up.
- **The standard for children's bed rails.** Since 2012, the US Consumer
  Product Safety Commission (CPSC) has required that a portable bed rail
  must not create a dangerous gap with the mattress into which a child can
  fall
  ([CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2012/CPSC-Adopts-New-Federal-Standard-for-Portable-Bed-Rails)).
  The principle is the same, but the object is different.
- **Locks on wall beds.** In Quebec in 2024, a coroner called for locking
  mechanisms on wall beds after a bed had opened onto a child
  ([Radio-Canada](https://ici.radio-canada.ca/rci/en/news/2106060/quebec-coroner-urges-canada-to-recall-murphy-beds-without-safeguards-after-death-of-5-year-old)).
  That addresses the opposite hazard, the bed falling open, not sliding into
  the gap.
- **Finland's current approach** is instructions for use and replacing the
  risky models. Reporting on daycare guidelines mentions no technical add-ons
  ([Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20218306)).

I did not find a product, standard or recommendation that closes the gap
between a fold-up wall bed and the wall with a barrier that moves with the
bed, nor a retrofit solution for the risky models. That is where the novelty
lies: the gap-closing principle familiar from hospital beds and children's
products, transferred to a moving bed and offered as a retrofit, as an
alternative to replacing the whole bed.

## Theoretical value

### Without the idea, the risky models get replaced anyway

A lot will happen in Finland in the coming years regardless: Vantaa is
replacing its risky models, Helsinki is giving up wall beds entirely, and
other municipalities have gone over their guidelines again. The baseline is
therefore a situation where the most dangerous beds have already been
replaced and the rest rely on instructions. The idea's value comes from two
differences: a retrofit may be cheaper than a new bed, and the remaining
risk from human error shrinks.

### Fermi estimate: roughly $0.2–2.5 million per year

| Factor | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Vantaa's cost of replacing risky models | approx. €220,000 | [Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20230874) |
| Children in early childhood education in Finland (2023) | 235,300 | [Statistics Finland](https://stat.fi/fi/tilasto/vaka) |
| Vantaa's share of the national total | 4–6 % | own assumption |
| Retrofit savings compared with replacement | 30–70 % | own assumption |
| Replacement cost spread over | 10 years | own assumption |
| Slides into the gap before any action | approx. 0.4 / year | derived from the Tukes and Otkes cases |
| Remaining risk after instructions and replacements | 10–30 % | own assumption |
| Share resulting in death | 10–25 % | own assumption |
| Value of a prevented death | $3–4 million | own assumption |
| Multiplier for other countries | 1–5 | own assumption |
| Euro to dollar rate | €1 ≈ $1.15 | own assumption |

- **Cost savings:** €220,000 / 5 % ≈ €4.4 million across Finland; 30–70 %
  savings on that is €1.3–3.1 million, or about $0.15–0.35 million per year
  when spread over ten years.
- **Safety benefit:** 0.4 × 10–30 % × 10–25 % ≈ 0.004–0.03 prevented deaths
  per year, or about $0.01–0.1 million per year. Serious injuries would come
  on top of this; I did not estimate them separately.
- **Total:** about $0.2–0.5 million per year in Finland, and with the
  multiplier for other countries roughly **$0.2–2.5 million per year**.

This is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a forecast.

### What moves it most is how common wall beds are elsewhere

I found no data on how many fold-up beds are in daycare centres outside
Finland, so the last multiplier is a pure guess. The other big factor is the
baseline: if municipalities replace the risky models quickly anyway, the cost
savings stay small. In money terms, then, the idea's value is small, because
the problem is rare and the baseline already fixes most of it. Its real
value is the principle: a single person's mistake should not be the only
thing standing between a child and death.
