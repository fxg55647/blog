The law today punishes stalking, threats and spreading lies, but deliberate,
long-running spite easily slips through if no single act is enough for any of
these. Picture a video maker whose whole channel is about mocking and making
life harder for one ordinary person, week after week, and who earns ad money
from it. I propose a new, general offence of spiteful harassment: it would be
a crime to act in a way whose clear main purpose is to torment someone else
and which has no other sensible purpose. Making money from the harassment
would not be a defence; if anything, it would make the act worse. At the same
time, ordinary criticism, journalism and satire must be clearly excluded so
the law does not become a muzzle on free speech.

*This is an unfinished working draft: the statutory text is a sketch, not a
polished proposal.*

## Punishability must not depend on the platform or the money

If the goal is specifically a general harassment offence that also covers
harassment done for profit, for example on YouTube, the provision should be
worded so that punishability does not depend on the platform or on whether
the perpetrator earns money from it. Commercial gain would instead be an
explicit aggravating factor.

### The principle of legality demands precision

In Finland such a provision has to be quite precise because of the principle
of legality in criminal law: punishable behaviour must be foreseeable from
the law. That is why the current Criminal Code defines, for example, stalking
and defamation through concrete acts and effects. Stalking covers someone who
*repeatedly threatens, follows, monitors, contacts or in some comparable way
unlawfully stalks another* so that it is likely to cause fear or distress
(Criminal Code 25:7 a, [Minilex](https://www.minilex.fi/node/59735)).
Harassment that does not frighten but annoys, humiliates and obstructs does
not always fit.

The Supreme Court has also held that not all feedback, however forceful, for
example to public officials, is punishable, and that stalking requires
repeated acts aimed at the same person
([KKO 2022:9, commentary](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/80996)).

## Draft: Spiteful harassment

> **Spiteful harassment**
>
> A person who intentionally acts towards another person in a manner
>
> 1. whose main purpose, clearly apparent from the circumstances, is to cause
>    that person harm, trouble, disturbance, humiliation, distress or another
>    comparable negative consequence,
> 2. which, assessed as a whole, has no other significant, acceptable and
>    independent purpose, and
> 3. which, considering its triviality, occasional nature or other
>    circumstances, cannot be regarded as ordinary or tolerable conduct
>    belonging to human interaction,
>
> shall be sentenced for spiteful harassment to a fine.
>
> Spiteful harassment may include, in particular, repeated contact,
> following, disturbing, making pointless reports or demands, systematically
> obstructing the other person's activities, or producing, publishing or
> distributing material concerning them, if the conduct meets the conditions
> in subsection 1.
>
> The commercial nature of the conduct, the financial gain derived from it or
> the gaining of an audience through it does not constitute an acceptable
> purpose if the main content or purpose of the activity is harassing another
> person as referred to in subsection 1.
>
> When assessing the purpose of the conduct, particular account shall be
> taken of the content, repetition and duration of the acts, the
> perpetrator's previous conduct, the targeting of the same person, the
> perpetrator's statements about their purpose, and whether the conduct had a
> genuine purpose independent of the harassment.
>
> Spiteful harassment does not, however, include justified criticism,
> expressing opinions, satire, journalism, scientific or artistic activity,
> consumer feedback, use of legal remedies, a justified report to an
> authority, or other exercise of a fundamental right or legal right, unless
> the form of activity is evidently used merely as a pretext for harassment.
>
> If a more severe penalty for the act is provided elsewhere in law, that
> provision shall apply primarily.

## Ad revenue does not make harassment acceptable

The subsection on commercial activity would matter precisely in YouTube,
TikTok, podcast and streaming cases. It would not be enough as a defence to
say: "I make these videos to get views and ad revenue." If the actual concept
of the videos is deliberately harassing one person, the commercial motive
would not turn it into an acceptable purpose.

### A critical video is not a crime because it makes money

At the same time, an ordinary critical YouTube video would not be a crime
merely because its maker earns money from it. If the video has a genuine
journalistic, societal, entertainment, critical or other independent purpose,
the offence would in principle not be made out. This exception matters for
free speech. The current defamation provision also explicitly protects
criticism of another person's conduct in, for example, politics, business,
public office, science or art that does not clearly exceed what is acceptable
(Criminal Code 24:9, subsection 2,
[Minilex](https://www.minilex.fi/a/rikoslaki-ja-kunnianloukkaus)).

## Next step: a basic and an aggravated form

A more workable legal model would split the offence into basic spiteful
harassment and aggravated spiteful harassment. In the aggravated form, for
example, systematic commercial harassment, a large audience, long duration
or substantial financial gain could raise the maximum penalty from a fine to
imprisonment.

## Open questions: can the target avoid it, and is online different?

Two more angles could be added to the draft:

- **Can the target avoid the situation?** The factors to be assessed could
  include whether the person harassed had a reasonable chance to avoid the
  conduct. You can block messages, but you cannot make videos about yourself
  disappear, and you cannot move out of your own home because of a
  neighbour's spite.
- **Online versus in-person harassment.** The law could address separately
  how harassment online differs from harassment elsewhere, for example in
  audience size, permanence of the material and how it spreads.

## Novelty

I searched for counterparts in the laws of Finland, the other Nordic
countries, France and Germany and in Finnish legislative drafting, using
terms such as *kiusanteko rikos*, *maalittaminen rikoslaki*, *hensynsløs
atferd*, *harcèlement moral en ligne* and *Schikaneverbot*.

### Norway already has a very similar general provision

The closest counterpart is **section 266 of the Norwegian Penal Code**
(*hensynsløs atferd*, reckless conduct): anyone who by frightening or
annoying behaviour or other reckless conduct pursues another person or
otherwise violates another's peace is liable to a fine or up to two years'
imprisonment ([ung.no](https://www.ung.no/oss/DGk37CFLCvf1wUsvK3CAP8)). In
September 2025 the Norwegian Supreme Court upheld a conviction under the
section for a Facebook post meant to damage another person's professional
standing
([HR-2025-1726-A](https://www.domstol.no/globalassets/upload/hret/avgjorelser/2025/september/hr-2025-1726.pdf)).
So a general harassment offence is not unheard of in the Nordics. The
Norwegian section, however, lacks two key parts of the draft: an explicit
rule that a commercial motive is not an acceptable purpose, and a list of
protected forms of activity.

### France addresses online harassment, Germany the "sole purpose"

- **Article 222-33-2-2 of the French Penal Code** punishes repeated
  harassment that degrades the victim's living conditions. Committing it
  online is an aggravating factor, and a concerted "raid" by several people
  counts as harassment even if no one acts repeatedly on their own
  ([LAGBD](https://lagbd.org/Cyber_harcelement_et_action_penale)). This
  matches the draft's open question about the online dimension, but does
  not deal with commercial motives.
- **Section 226 of the German Civil Code** (*Schikaneverbot*) prohibits
  exercising a right if its only possible purpose is to harm another
  ([buzer.de](https://www.buzer.de/226_BGB.htm)). The idea of "no other
  acceptable purpose" is therefore old, but in civil law, not criminal law.

In Finland, recent legislative work has focused on, among other things,
coercive control in close relationships
([Verkkouutiset 2023](https://www.verkkouutiset.fi/a/rikoslakiin-pitaisi-lisata-vainoamisen-rinnalle-uusi-rikosnimike-ehdottaa-selvitys/),
[LA 35/2025](https://www.eduskunta.fi/pdf/LA+35/2025)). I did not find a
Finnish proposal for a general harassment offence. Nor did I find a provision
in any country that states directly that ad revenue or an audience gained
from harassment does not make it acceptable. The novelty lies precisely in
this commercial rule and in combining it with a Norwegian-style general
provision that has a purpose test precise enough for Finnish law and a free
speech exception.

## Theoretical value

### Without the law, platforms and existing offences handle part of it

The baseline is the near future without the new offence. AI-based moderation
on platforms will improve anyway, EU platform regulation requires action on
illegal content, and the most serious harassment is already punishable as
stalking, illegal threats or defamation. The new offence is worth only the
harm that falls between these and that the law would prevent through
deterrence: harassment that neither frightens nor lies but humiliates and
obstructs, and is often allowed under platform rules.

### Fermi estimate: roughly $0.2–18 billion a year

Assume all wealthy democracies adopted such an offence.

| Factor | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of gender-based cyber violence in the EU | €49–89 bn / yr ≈ $54–98 bn | European Parliamentary Research Service 2021 ([EPRS](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/thinktank/en/document/EPRS_STU(2021)662621), annual basis: [EIGE](https://eige.europa.eu/newsroom/director-corner/building-safe-digital-spaces-women-and-girls-click-cyber-violence)) |
| Extension to all genders and in-person harassment | × 1.5–2.5 | own assumption |
| Extension from the EU to all wealthy democracies | × 3–5 | own assumption |
| Share of harm falling between existing offences and moderation | 5–15 % | own assumption |
| Deterrent effect of the law on that share | 2–10 % | own assumption |

Total harm from harassment would thus be roughly $240–1,200 billion a year.
As a sanity check on scale: in the United States, 41% of adults have
experienced online harassment and 25% more severe harassment
([Pew 2021](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2021/01/13/the-state-of-online-harassment/)).

- Lower bound: $240 bn × 5% × 2% ≈ **$0.2 bn / year**
- Upper bound: $1,200 bn × 15% × 10% ≈ **$18 bn / year**

The range is therefore roughly **$0.2–18 billion a year**. An
order-of-magnitude estimate, not a forecast.

### What swings it most: whether a fine deters anyone

The deterrent effect swings the result most. A fine alone may not deter a
perpetrator whose channel earns more than the fine costs. That is why the
aggravated form and the commercial rule are the most important parts of the
draft in terms of value. Pulling the other way is the risk that an imprecise
provision would make people avoid legitimate criticism too. If the free
speech exception does not work in practice, the net value could fall to the
lower bound or below zero. The figure also excludes the cost of extra work
for police and courts.
